📚 VOCABULARY

Event: A result or outcome that can occur in a probability experiment. Events can be simple, like rolling a 3 on a six-sided die, or complex, involving the combination of multiple simple events.

Experimental Probability: The probability of an event determined through actual experimentation and observation. It is calculated by dividing the number of times an event occurs by the total number of trials in the experiment.

Fairness: The equal likelihood of outcomes in a probability experiment. A game or experiment is considered fair if all possible outcomes have the same chance of happening.

Outcome: The result of a single trial of a probability experiment. For instance, if you flip a coin, the outcome can be either heads or tails.

Sample Size: The number of observations or trials in an experiment. It is an important factor in determining the reliability of experimental results. Larger sample sizes generally lead to more reliable estimates of probability.

Sample Space: The set of all possible outcomes of a probability experiment. For example, the sample space for rolling a six-sided die is {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}.

Simple Experiment: A probability experiment with a single action that results in a definite outcome. Flipping a coin once is an example of a simple experiment.

Simulation: A model or representation of a situation used to study the possible outcomes of an experiment without conducting the experiment in real life. Simulations often use random numbers to mimic the process of an experiment.

Theoretical Probability: The probability of an event calculated based on the possible outcomes in the sample space, without conducting any actual experiments. It is determined by dividing the number of favorable outcomes by the total number of possible outcomes in the sample space.

In today's lesson, we will explore the exciting world of probabilities. Specifically, we'll look at the differences between theoretical and experimental probabilities through simulated experiments. By the end of this lesson, you'll understand why these differences occur and how they can change based on the number of trials.


We will begin by reviewing theoretical probability versus experimental probability. Then watch a short video on comparing these probabilities in a simple experiment.

Theoretical vs. Experimental Probability

First, let's define our terms:

  • Theoretical Probability is what we expect to happen. It's calculated based on the possible outcomes of an event. For example, the theoretical probability of flipping a coin and getting heads is 1/2, because there are two possible outcomes, and one of them is heads.

  • Experimental Probability is what actually happens when we conduct an experiment. It's calculated by dividing the number of times an event occurs by the total number of trials.

Theoretical probability gives us an expectation, while experimental probability provides us with real-world results. Over many trials, experimental probabilities tend to get closer to theoretical probabilities, illustrating the Law of Large Numbers.


Connecting Theoretical and Experimental Probability

Theoretical probability helps us predict the outcomes before we conduct an experiment. When we perform an experiment multiple times, we can compare the experimental probability to the theoretical probability. To get experimental probabilities closer to theoretical probabilities, we can increase the number of trials in our experiments.


Now we will look at why there are differences in the theoretical probability and the experimental probability and the law of large numbers while exploring an example of a simple experiment. We will explore the experiment at 10 trials, 100, trials, and 1000 trials.


Why Differences Occur

You might wonder why there's a difference between theoretical and experimental probabilities. The main reason is randomness. Each trial in an experiment is subject to change, which means the outcome can vary. However, as we increase the number of trials, the experimental probability tends to get closer to the theoretical probability. This phenomenon is explained by the Law of Large Numbers.


The Law of Large Numbers

The Law of Large Numbers tells us that the more trials we conduct, the closer our experimental probability will get to the theoretical probability. This is because the effects of randomness even out over many trials, leading to a more accurate representation of the probability.


Simulated Experiment: Flipping a Coin

Let's simulate flipping a coin to better understand these concepts.

10 Trials vs. 100 Trials vs. 1000 Trials

  • 10 Trials: With only 10 flips, you might get 6 heads and 4 tails. The experimental probability of flipping heads is 6/10 or 60%. This is quite different from the theoretical probability of 50%.
  • 100 Trials: With more flips, say you get 48 heads and 52 tails. The experimental probability of flipping heads is now closer to theoretical, at 48%.
  • 1000 Trials: With even more flips, suppose you get 498 heads. The experimental probability of flipping heads becomes 49.8%, very close to the theoretical 50%.


Understanding Discrepancies

As you can see, the discrepancy between the theoretical and experimental probabilities decreases as the number of trials increases. This illustrates the Law of Large Numbers in action. The randomness inherent in small sample sizes can cause significant deviations from the expected outcome, but as the sample size grows, the experimental probability converges towards the theoretical probability.

Conclusion

The comparison between theoretical and experimental probabilities, especially through simulated experiments, helps us understand the nature of chance and probability. It shows us that while randomness can lead to discrepancies in the short term, the long-run frequency over many trials gives us a clearer picture that aligns with our theoretical expectations. This lesson emphasizes the importance of sample size in experiments and demonstrates why patience and a large number of trials are crucial for reaching accurate conclusions in the world of probability. Keep experimenting, and you'll see the Law of Large Numbers come to life!
