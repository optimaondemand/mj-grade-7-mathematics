📚 VOCABULARY

Categorical Data: Data that can be grouped into categories based on shared characteristics or qualities.

Circle Graph: A visual representation of data in the form of a circle divided into sections, showing parts of a whole.

Percentage: A way of expressing a number as a fraction of 100.

Population: The entire group of individuals or items being studied or surveyed.

Predictions: An estimate about what might happen in the future based on data or trends.

Proportional Relationships: A relationship where two quantities change at the same rate.

Random Sampling: A method of selecting a sample where every individual or item has an equal chance of being chosen.

Sample Population: A smaller group selected from the population to represent the whole.

Segment: A part of something, often referring to sections of data or graphs.

Visual Representation: A way of displaying information using images, charts, or graphs.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

In today's lesson, we will explore how to analyze categorical data from a random sample to understand the characteristics of a larger population. By looking at patterns in the data, we can use proportional relationships to make predictions about the population as a whole. This skill is important because it allows us to draw conclusions and make decisions based on smaller, manageable sets of data. We’ll use bar charts, tables, and written descriptions to practice these concepts.


Understanding Categorical Data

Categorical data groups information into categories. For example, if a survey asks people about their favorite ice cream flavors, the responses could be grouped into categories like chocolate, vanilla, and strawberry.

Using Random Sampling

A random sample is a smaller group selected randomly from a population. It provides a snapshot of the larger group. For instance, surveying 50 students about their favorite ice cream flavor gives us a sample that can represent all students in the school.

Proportional Relationships and Predictions

Proportional relationships allow us to use data from the sample to predict characteristics of the whole population. If \(\frac{20}{100}\) or 20% of a sample prefer chocolate ice cream, we can predict that \(\frac{20}{100}\) or 20% of the population will also prefer chocolate.



Example 1

A survey of 60 students asked about their favorite types of pets. The results are shown in the bar chart below.

If the total school population is 600 students, how many students would you predict have dogs as their favorite pet?

Solution:

  • Calculate the proportion of the sample: \(\frac{30}{60}\)
  • Multiply by the total population by the sample proportion: \(\frac{30}{60}\cdot600=\frac{30\cdot600}{60}=\frac{18000}{60}=300\)
  • Prediction: \(300\) students prefer dogs.



Example 2

A population of 63 people was surveyed and asked what food they liked best from the list {Pizza, Burgers, or Salads}. The bar chart shows the results of the survey.

  1. Approximately what percentage of the population could be predicted to like burgers if the total population is 552?
  2. About how many people might prefer salads if the population is 552?

Solution:

  • For #1
    • Calculate the proportion of the sample that like burgers: \(\frac{20}{63}\)
    • The proportion that like burgers in the sample will be the same as the total population. So, we can use the fraction to calculate the percentage and it will be the same percentage for the total.
    • Approximate percentage that like burgers \(=\frac{20}{63}\approx0.3175\cdot100\approx31.75\%\)
  • For #2
    • First calculate the proportion of the sample that prefers salads: \(\frac{18}{63}\)
    • Multiply the proportion of the sample by the total population: \(\frac{18}{63}\cdot552=\frac{18\cdot552}{63}=\frac{9936}{63}\approx157.7\approx158\) people



Example 3

A random sample of 40 students was asked about their favorite school subjects. The data is shown in the table.

Subject Number of Students
Math 10
Science 15
History 8
English 7

If the total population is 650 students, approximately how many students in the population would prefer

  • Math
  • Science
  • History
  • English

Solution:

To calculate approximately how many students would prefer each subject of the total population, we need to first calculate the proportion from the sample and multiply it by the total population.

  • Math \(=\frac{10}{40}\cdot650=\frac{10\cdot650}{40}=\frac{6500}{40}=162.5\) students
  • Science \(=\frac{15}{40}\cdot650=\frac{15\cdot650}{40}=\frac{9750}{40}=243.75\) students
  • History \(=\frac{8}{40}\cdot650=\frac{8\cdot650}{40}=\frac{5200}{40}=130\) students
  • English \(=\frac{7}{40}\cdot650=\frac{7\cdot650}{40}=\frac{4550}{40}=113.75\) students



Example 4

A marine research team is studying the dolphin population in a coastal region. The team identified 12 tagged dolphins among 96 dolphins observed during a survey.

The team knows that a total of 50 dolphins in the region have been tagged.

How many dolphins can the research team predict are in the coastal region? Round to the nearest whole number if necessary.

Solution:

To find the population of dolphins if 50 are tagged using the sample proportion, we need to first find the proportion of the sample.

  • \(\frac{12}{96}\)

Next, we will use the proportion to create a proportion of \(\frac{tagged}{population\;size}\).

  • \(\frac{50}{x}=\frac{12}{96}\)

Finally, we will solve the proportion using cross-multiplication.

  • \(12\cdot(x)=50\cdot96\)
    \(12x=4800\)
    \(x=\frac{4800}{12}=400\)

The research team can predict that there are 400 dolphins in the coastal region.


Conclusion

Analyzing categorical data helps us understand trends and make predictions about a population. By using proportional relationships, we can turn small samples into meaningful insights about larger groups. Practice analyzing bar charts, tables, and written descriptions to strengthen your skills. This ability to interpret data is useful in many real-world situations, from understanding survey results to making informed decisions.
